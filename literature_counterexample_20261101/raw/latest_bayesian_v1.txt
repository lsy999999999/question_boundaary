Abstract

Scientific model discovery requires proposing candidate models of an unknown system and designing experiments that discriminate among them. However, LLM-based approaches typically rely on in-context learning to predict outcomes and select experiments, lacking explicit model evaluation to systematically guide hypothesis search, while Bayesian approaches enable principled model comparison but are confined to a predefined candidate set which results in limited search spaces.
To this end, we introduce Bayesian Model Discovery (BMD), which closes the loop between LLM-driven hypothesis generation and Bayesian experimental design.
Specifically, an LLM proposes model families, while a numerical engine fits their parameters, weighs them by model evidence, and averages their predictions.
Then, BMD chooses the next experimental conditions by maximizing the joint expected information gain over families and their parameters, while feeding the updated beliefs and residual diagnostics back to the LLM to guide its next proposals.
Across four scientific discovery benchmarks, BMD achieves lower final prediction error than in-context learning and LLM-based scientific agents while recovering exact functional forms more often, with ablations supporting numerical feedback and the joint objective.

1 Introduction

Many scientific advances take the form of mathematical models that describe how a system responds to experimental conditions (Langley et al., 1987; Schmidt and Lipson, 2009; Wang et al., 2023).
Discovering such models alternates between proposing candidate models and designing experiments that discriminate among them (Box and Hill, 1967; Blei, 2014).
For example, a scientist studying how a drug slows down an enzyme may consider two candidate models: the drug either competes with the molecule the enzyme acts on or attaches elsewhere on the enzyme.
Both models may fit the initial measurements equally well, but the scientist can tell them apart with an experiment that adds a large amount of this molecule, as the two predict different reaction rates.
Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown promise in automating this process (Boiko et al., 2023; Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024; Shojaee et al., 2025; Baek et al., 2025), owing to their broad scientific knowledge and reasoning abilities.

One common approach is in-context learning (ICL) (Brown et al., 2020), where the LLM directly predicts responses and selects experiments from observations (Gandhi et al., 2025).
While this approach exploits the prior knowledge of LLMs, it does not commit to explicit models fitted to data, leaving the LLM to make every prediction and choose experiments without quantifying which explanations remain plausible (Gupta et al., 2025).

In contrast, explicit probabilistic models address both issues by expressing each candidate explanation as an equation, called a model family, whose functional form (e.g., competitive inhibition) contains unknown parameters (e.g., the maximum reaction rate).
Specifically, once these parameters are estimated, the equation can predict responses at untested conditions, allowing a suitable family to generalize from few observations.
In addition, these models guide experiment selection, as Bayesian inference quantifies how plausible each family and its parameters remain (Kass and Raftery, 1995; Hoeting et al., 1999), which Bayesian experimental design (Lindley, 1956; Chaloner and Verdinelli, 1995; Rainforth et al., 2024) uses to select the most informative experiment to run next.

Given the observations 𝒟t\mathcal{D}_{t}, the numerical engine fits each candidate family and returns the resulting beliefs and residual diagnostics to the LLM, which proposes new families.
These proposals are validated, fitted, and compared with the existing families, after which the updated pool selects the next experimental conditions 𝐱t+1\mathbf{x}_{t+1} by maximizing the joint expected information gain.

However, explicit modeling alone is not sufficient, as Gandhi et al. (2025) find no consistent benefit from augmenting an LLM with a statistical model.
Moreover, Bayesian inference over a fixed set of candidates cannot recover a family missing from it, while LLMs can propose new families (Li et al., 2024; Shojaee et al., 2025) that remain unverified until they are fitted against the data.
The central challenge, therefore, lies in connecting family search, parameter inference, and experiment selection such that each step informs the others.

To address this, we present Bayesian Model Discovery (BMD), a framework that closes the loop between LLM-driven hypothesis generation and Bayesian experimental design (Figure 1).
Our key insight is that LLMs and Bayesian inference have complementary strengths: LLMs can draw on domain knowledge to propose plausible functional forms, whereas Bayesian inference can quantify how well each form explains the data.
Specifically, BMD employs an LLM to propose model families, while a numerical engine fits their parameters, weighs each family by its model evidence, and averages their predictions.
Then, since a new observation can distinguish competing families, refine their parameters, or both, BMD chooses the next experimental conditions by maximizing the joint expected information gain over families and their parameters (Borth, 1975; McGree, 2017), while feeding the updated beliefs and residual diagnostics back to the LLM to inform its next proposals.

We validate BMD on four scientific discovery benchmarks, namely ChemBench (Kabra et al., 2026), BoxingGym (Gandhi et al., 2025), ForceBench (Wiemann et al., 2026), and NewtonBench (Zheng et al., 2026).
BMD achieves the lowest final prediction error on every benchmark against direct ICL and recent LLM-based scientific agents, while also recovering exact functional forms more often.
Our analyses further show that numerical feedback to the LLM improves prediction and that the joint objective outperforms targeting either families or parameters alone.
These findings suggest that LLMs are most effective as hypothesis generators within a closed loop of explicit model fitting and experimental design, rather than as standalone predictors of experimental results.

2 Related Work

LLM-assisted model discovery.

Symbolic regression searches for interpretable equations from
observations (Schmidt and Lipson, 2009; Brunton et al., 2016; Cranmer, 2023).
LLM-SR combines language-model proposals with numerical parameter
optimization (Shojaee et al., 2025), while BoxLM iterates
probabilistic-program generation, fitting, and criticism (Li et al., 2024).
Other methods weight LLM-proposed models by their marginal
likelihood (Domke, 2025; Wahl et al., 2026).
Box’s Apprentice and LLM-AutoSciLab extend
model-based reasoning to interactive experimentation
 (Gandhi et al., 2025; Kabra et al., 2026).
Other closed-loop approaches select experiments by information gain over
LLM-proposed hypotheses, such as natural-language rules
 (Piriyakulkij et al., 2024) or cognitive models (Prystawski et al., 2026).
BMD searches over functional-form families using numerical parameter
posteriors and evidence-weighted predictions.

Bayesian experimental design.

Bayesian experimental design selects observations to reduce
uncertainty (Lindley, 1956; Chaloner and Verdinelli, 1995; Rainforth et al., 2024). Established criteria include
joint model discrimination and parameter estimation
 (Hill et al., 1968; Borth, 1975; McGree, 2017), and
expected predictive information gain (EPIG) (Bickford Smith et al., 2023).
BMD uses joint EIG within an evolving pool of LLM-proposed families to
value both model discrimination and parameter refinement. We also
evaluate EPIG, which instead targets predictions at inputs of interest.
Recent work also applies EIG to LLM question asking, but estimates it
from the predictive distribution of the LLM itself rather than from fitted models
 (Choudhury et al., 2026).

Concurrent work: Model Discovery Agent.

Model Discovery Agent (MDA; Murphy, 2026) similarly combines
LLM proposals, Bayesian inference, adaptive experimentation, and
numerical feedback. The cited MDA implementation uses a variance-based
surrogate for EIG, including a parameter-aware variant. BMD instead
estimates joint model–parameter EIG from fitted predictive likelihoods.
A second difference is the context supplied to the proposer. MDA
conditions proposals on domain context, whereas we anonymize variable
names and withhold task descriptions and semantic metadata on ChemBench,
BoxingGym, and NewtonBench. Numerical ranges and the modeling interface
remain available; ForceBench retains physical task metadata
(Section 4). This evaluates discovery from
observations and feedback with fewer supplied domain cues.

3 Approach

Our Bayesian Model Discovery (BMD) combines the flexibility of LLMs in proposing diverse functional forms with the strengths of Bayesian inference in evaluating hypotheses and quantifying uncertainty. By integrating these complementary capabilities, BMD iteratively discovers and refines scientific models through hypothesis generation, numerical evaluation, and experimental design (Figure 1;
Algorithm 1).

3.1 Problem setup and overview

An experimental design 𝐱∈𝒳\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X} specifies controllable
conditions, where
𝒳\mathcal{X} is the set of allowed designs. The response yy
is the outcome measured under design 𝐱\mathbf{x} and can be multi-dimensional.

A model family mm specifies a functional form, such as a linear or saturating function. 𝜽m\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m} denotes a parameter vector associated with mm. We assume that likelihood family is fixed for each task (e.g., Gaussian). mm, 𝜽m\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}, and likelihood together define the conditional response distribution pm​(y∣𝐱,𝜽m)p_{m}(y\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}), expressed as a probability density or mass function. For each task, the true family m⋆m^{\star} and its parameter vector 𝜽⋆\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star} remain fixed and we can define

p⋆​(y∣𝐱):=pm⋆​(y∣𝐱,𝜽⋆).p^{\star}(y\mid\mathbf{x}):=p_{m^{\star}}(y\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}).

The agent observes samples from this distribution but is not given
m⋆m^{\star} or 𝜽⋆\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}.

The initial dataset 𝒟0\mathcal{D}_{0} contains n0n_{0} observed
design–response pairs. With a budget of BB additional experiments,
at round t=1,…,Bt=1,\ldots,B, the agent chooses 𝐱t∈𝒳\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{X} using
𝒟t−1\mathcal{D}_{t-1} and observes yt∼p⋆(⋅∣𝐱t)y_{t}\sim p^{\star}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}).
The available data are then
𝒟t=𝒟0∪{(𝐱s,ys)}s=1t\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{0}\cup\{(\mathbf{x}_{s},y_{s})\}_{s=1}^{t}.
The goals are accurate prediction at untested designs as well as recovery of
the underlying true functional form m⋆m^{\star} and its parameter 𝜽⋆\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}.

3.2 Parameter inference and model evidence

Unlike previous ICL-based approaches, our BMD separates the roles of the LLM and numerical inference. The LLM proposes candidate model families, while the numerical engine estimates the parameter posterior and model evidence for each candidate.

We first discuss posterior inference of model parameters within each candidate model family. We specify a prior πm​(𝜽m)\pi_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}) using
fixed coefficient-role rules.
Examples include 𝒩⁡(0,1)\mathcal{N}(0,1) for signed coefficients and
LogNormal⁡(0,1)\operatorname{LogNormal}(0,1) for positive coefficients.
The numerical engine assumes conditional independence of responses
given their designs and parameters, giving the likelihood

(1)

Then, Bayes’ rule yields the parameter posterior and its normalizing
constant Zm,t=p⁡(𝒟t∣m)Z_{m,t}=p(\mathcal{D}_{t}\mid m), called the model evidence:

p⁡(𝜽m∣𝒟t,m)\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}\mid\mathcal{D}_{t},m)
=πm​(𝜽m)​Lm,t​(𝜽m)Zm,t,\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m})L_{m,t}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m})}{Z_{m,t}},

(2)

=∫πm​(𝜽m)​Lm,t​(𝜽m)​d​𝜽m.\displaystyle=\int\pi_{m}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m})L_{m,t}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}.

The posterior p⁡(𝜽m∣𝒟t,m)p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}\mid\mathcal{D}_{t},m) describes the uncertainty of 𝜽m\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m} within the family mm, given the data 𝒟t\mathcal{D}_{t}. The evidence Zm,tZ_{m,t}
measures its support by averaging the likelihood over the prior. Evidence thus

We use sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) (Del Moral et al., 2006) to obtain posterior samples and
an evidence estimate Z^m,t\widehat{Z}_{m,t}. Those posterior samples and evidence estimate are the core ingredients of BMD, which we explain in the next subsections. Appendix B gives the numerical settings.

3.3 Model comparison and prediction

We next discuss posterior inference over candidate model families to assess which functional forms are more plausible given the observations. For the retained pool ℳt\mathcal{M}_{t}, equal family-level prior weights give the posterior model probabilities

wm,t=Z^m,t∑j∈ℳtZ^j,t,m∈ℳt,w_{m,t}=\frac{\widehat{Z}_{m,t}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{t}}\widehat{Z}_{j,t}},\qquad m\in\mathcal{M}_{t},

These probabilities are defined within the current fixed-sized model pool, rather than over all possible functional forms. Restricting inference to a finite pool makes model comparison tractable, although the true family need not belong to the pool. Section 3.5 explains how the pool
evolves through LLM-generated proposals.

averages its likelihood over its posterior, giving the

3.4 Information-based experimental design

BMD selects experiments using joint expected information gain (EIG),
which targets uncertainty in both the model family and its parameters.

Joint model–parameter EIG.

For a candidate design 𝐱\mathbf{x}, let MM be a random family drawn from
ℳt\mathcal{M}_{t} with probabilities wm,tw_{m,t}. Conditional on M=mM=m, draw
its parameter vector 𝚯M\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{M} from
p⁡(𝜽m∣𝒟t,m)p(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}\mid\mathcal{D}_{t},m), and then draw a hypothetical response
Y𝐱Y_{\mathbf{x}} from pM(⋅∣𝐱,𝚯M)p_{M}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{M}).
Its marginal distribution is qt(⋅∣𝐱)q_{t}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}).
Using II for mutual information, joint EIG is

at​(𝐱)\displaystyle a_{t}(\mathbf{x})
=I⁡(Y𝐱;M,𝚯M∣𝒟t)\displaystyle=I(Y_{\mathbf{x}};M,\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{M}\mid\mathcal{D}_{t})

(6)

=𝔼⁡[log⁡pM​(Y𝐱∣𝐱,𝚯M)qt​(Y𝐱∣𝐱)],\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log\frac{p_{M}(Y_{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{M})}{q_{t}(Y_{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})}\right],

where the expectation is over these draws. The numerator evaluates
an outcome under one family and parameter setting; the denominator
averages over the alternatives. The expected log ratio, at​(𝐱)a_{t}(\mathbf{x}),
thus measures how informative an observation at 𝐱\mathbf{x} would be about
the model family and its parameters.

3.5 LLM-guided model pool expansion

The preceding sections describe parameter and model inference,
BMA prediction, and information-based experiment selection.
However, all these operations are defined within the current
model pool. If the true functional form is missing, updating
posteriors and collecting further observations do not by themselves
introduce it. Discovery therefore requires a mechanism for
proposing new functional forms.

Bayesian inference can be extended to search over symbolic expressions,
but such methods typically rely on a predefined grammar and can be
computationally expensive. BMD instead draws on an LLM’s learned
mathematical knowledge to propose new forms without exhaustively
enumerating expressions. Numerical feedback is supplied in context
to guide these proposals toward what the current candidates fail
to explain.

At each round t≥1t\geq 1, after acquiring a new observation,
the engine refits the families in ℳt−1\mathcal{M}_{t-1} on the
updated dataset 𝒟t\mathcal{D}_{t}.
The LLM then receives the accumulated observations,
candidate functional forms, and two types of numerical feedback:
belief information, including model evidence,
posterior model probabilities, parameter posterior summaries,
and one-step predictive log scores; and
diagnostics, including model-wise standardized residuals.
Belief information indicates which models the data support,
while diagnostics reveal where their predictions fall short.
For example, systematic curvature in a linear model’s residuals
may motivate a nonlinear alternative.
The LLM uses this feedback to propose new functional forms.

The engine validates and deduplicates the proposals and fits them on
the same 𝒟t\mathcal{D}_{t}. It retains up to KK highest-evidence
families from the existing and newly proposed candidates to form
ℳt\mathcal{M}_{t}, where KK is the pool capacity. Model probabilities and
BMA predictions are then recomputed, and the updated mixture guides
the next experiment. The resulting observation informs the next
round of numerical inference and LLM proposals, closing the loop
between hypothesis generation and experimentation.
Appendix B gives implementation details,
including periodic reconsideration of retired models;
Appendix G provides the prompts.

4 Experiments

We first evaluate BMD’s predictive accuracy and functional-form recovery,
then examine the source of performance improvements.

Benchmarks and baselines.

We use four simulated scientific discovery benchmarks:
ChemBench (Kabra et al., 2026),
BoxingGym (Gandhi et al., 2025),
ForceBench (Wiemann et al., 2026),
and NewtonBench (Zheng et al., 2026).
Table 1 summarizes these benchmarks.
For baselines, we first consider in-context learning
(ICL), which selects experiments and predicts
directly from observations without fitting explicit models.
Our primary baseline, LLM-AutoSciLab (Kabra et al., 2026),
maintains LLM-proposed hypotheses but fits them by regression
and selects experiments by prediction disagreement rather
than evidence and EIG.
We also compare with Box’s Apprentice (Gandhi et al., 2025)
and DP-Agent (Wiemann et al., 2026),
the reference agents of BoxingGym and ForceBench, respectively.
All methods use Claude Opus 5.5 for their LLM calls.

Information supplied to the agents.

BMD and all baselines receive the same numerical ranges of the
design variables, with variable names anonymized.
Task descriptions and semantic metadata are withheld.
This eliminates explicit domain-specific semantic cues,
allowing us to better assess model discovery driven primarily
by observations and numerical feedback.
The exception is ForceBench, which is specifically designed
to use physical task metadata. We retain this information
for all methods to preserve the intended benchmark setting.
Appendix A details the information and
observation interfaces for each agent and benchmark.

Evaluation protocol.

The main BMD runs use joint EIG. We report error against the total

Accurate prediction alone does not establish that an agent has
identified the underlying law. We therefore evaluate
strict recovery (solid lines), which requires certified
structural equivalence to the true family, and

a broader family capable of representing the true law.

At each observation budget, we evaluate BMD’s highest-evidence

Figure 3 reports the fraction of
runs that satisfy each criterion, denoted as Top-1 recovery rate.
Appendix C provides the evaluation details.

BMD demonstrates a clear advantage in exact-form discovery.
On BoxingGym, ForceBench, and NewtonBench, BMD successfully recovers

earlier and maintains higher strict recovery at the final budget.
BMD also achieves higher containment on BoxingGym and NewtonBench.

passes the strict criterion, whereas BMD achieves high recovery

4.3 The role of numerical model evaluation

BMD uses the LLM to propose model families but delegates
parameter fitting and model comparison to a numerical engine.
Complementing the end-to-end results in Figure 2, we test
whether this division of labor improves upon the LLM’s own
parameter estimates and model choices.

Experimental setup.

For each world–seed pair, we collect 20 random-design
observations. Using only the first 10, Claude Opus 5.5
proposes 11 alternative families, which we combine with
the ground-truth (GT) family into a fixed pool of K=12K=12
candidates. All methods share this pool and the full
observation set.

Models.

We consider the following methods.

• 

A (Direct ICL):
The LLM predicts responses directly from the observations
and the pool.

• 

B (LLM model selection + LLM parameters):
The LLM selects a family from the pool and specifies
its parameter values based on the observations; the resulting
model is used for prediction.

• 

C (LLM model selection + Bayesian parameter fitting):
Retains B’s selected family but infers its parameter
posterior using SMC and predicts with the posterior
predictive mean.

• 

D (Evidence-based model selection + Bayesian parameter fitting):
Selects the highest-evidence family among SMC-fitted
candidates and predicts with its posterior predictive mean.

B–C compares parameter inference within the same family,
while C–D compares model selection under the same SMC
parameter-fitting procedure.
We report error on shared held-out points and GT identification
by structural equivalence of the selected family.

Results.

Table 2 shows progressively lower
cross-benchmark geometric-mean error from A to B to C to D,
revealing three complementary benefits.

First, A–B shows that predicting through an explicit model
improves upon direct ICL, even when the LLM itself selects
the family and specifies its parameters.
Second, B–C shows that Bayesian parameter fitting further improves
prediction without changing the selected family,
highlighting the importance of numerical parameter inference.
Third, C–D shows that replacing LLM model selection with
evidence-based model selection improves aggregate prediction and
pooled top-1 GT identification (79.3% to 84.8%).

Overall, D reduces prediction error by a factor of 4.7
relative to A. These results show that predicting through
explicit models improves aggregate accuracy over direct ICL.
Once such models are introduced, however, selecting the
right family and fitting its parameters become critical.
Numerical parameter fitting and evidence-based selection
further improve performance over the LLM’s own estimates
and choices. This motivates BMD’s division of labor:
the LLM proposes functional forms, while the numerical
engine fits and compares them.

4.4 The value of joint EIG

An informative experiment may distinguish competing model
families, refine their parameters, or both. Figure 6 compares our Joint EIG acquisition function against Model-only EIG, Parameter-only EIG, and Random design (random selection of the next design point 𝐱t+1\mathbf{x}_{t+1}).
Model-only EIG and Parameter-only EIG correspond to the terms in

Joint EIG achieves the lowest final prediction error on both benchmarks.
On ChemBench, it maintains a clear advantage over the alternatives
through much of the later observation budgets.
On ForceBench, Joint EIG reduces prediction error substantially faster
at intermediate budgets, although the other methods eventually
approach its performance.
These results highlight the benefit of jointly targeting model
discrimination and parameter refinement, suggesting that
accounting for both sources of uncertainty can lead to
more effective experimental designs.

4.5 What feedback helps hypothesis discovery?

Finally, beyond fitting and comparing candidate models, BMD feeds
numerical inference results back to the LLM to guide
subsequent hypothesis generation.
We examine whether this feedback improves discovery through
a 2×22\times 2 ablation: Full (belief + diagnostics),
Belief-only, Diagnostics-only, and No feedback.
Belief feedback includes model evidence, posterior weights,
and parameter summaries, while diagnostics provide
model-wise predictive residuals.
All variants share the same initialization and retain
Bayesian inference and joint-EIG experimental design.

Figure 6 shows that Full achieves the lowest final prediction
error on both ChemBench and ForceBench, while No feedback
performs worst. Either feedback type generally improves
prediction, although their relative contributions vary
across benchmarks.
These results suggest that numerical inference is valuable
not only for evaluating existing hypotheses but also for
guiding the LLM toward better subsequent proposals,
supporting BMD’s closed-loop design.

5 Conclusion

BMD connects LLM hypothesis generation, Bayesian model
evaluation, and information-theoretic experimental design. Across four simulated benchmarks,
it improves prediction, sample efficiency, and exact-form
recovery. Controlled comparisons favor explicit modeling over
direct ICL, with further gains from Bayesian fitting and
evidence-based selection. Acquisition comparisons favor joint
model–parameter EIG, while feedback ablations show that
returning numerical beliefs and diagnostics to the LLM
improves downstream prediction, reinforcing BMD’s closed-loop design.

Limitations

Our experiments focus on simulated scientific tasks;
validation in real-world experimental settings remains
future work. Such evaluation would help assess robustness
beyond the observation models and experimental conditions
considered here. BMD’s uncertainty estimates are conditional
on its current finite candidate pool rather than the space
of all possible laws. Although the pool evolves through
LLM proposals, discovery still depends on generating suitable
model families, and our structural-recovery evaluation
covers only targets expressible in the modeling interface.

Ethical Considerations

Our experiments are conducted in simulated scientific
environments without human participants.
While BMD aims to assist scientific discovery, automatically
generated hypotheses may be inaccurate or misleading,
potentially leading to incorrect scientific conclusions.
Real-world applications should therefore involve independent
validation of discovered models and appropriate human oversight
before conducting consequential experiments.

Each trajectory contains two coordinates at ten measurement times.
No world name, force law, or law catalogue is supplied to the proposer.

Section 4.3 is the explicit exception: it inserts the GT family

Baseline interfaces and schedules.

All methods use Claude Opus 5.5 for their LLM calls.
LLM-AutoSciLab uses an every-round variant for predictive curves:
one experiment and one equation-learning step per round, with the same
LLM and reasoning effort as BMD. It follows the same anonymization and
metadata policy described above: in particular, it does not receive
native variable names or chemical task metadata on ChemBench.
Its NewtonBench recovery curve uses the released schedule, with the
first law at n=16n=16.

The ForceBench comparisons use the rerun with a common information and
observation interface for BMD, direct ICL, LLM-AutoSciLab, and DP-Agent.
All receive the same physical task metadata and design constraints,
observe trajectories through the same interface, and are evaluated
using the same targets and scorer. Each trajectory counts as one
observation for every method. The agents may choose different designs
and hence observe different responses, but they do not receive different
types of experimental measurements.

Box’s Apprentice on BoxingGym and DP-Agent on ForceBench
also use every-round variants. Direct ICL chooses experimental
designs from its observation history and receives separate prediction
requests at each observation count; these requests do not change that
history. Different experiment-selection policies are part of the
comparison, whereas Section 4.3 holds the realized observations
and candidate families fixed across methods.

Appendix B Numerical Implementation

Model representation and validation.

Each candidate specifies a functional expression and its coefficient
roles and domains. As in Section 3.1, the observation-distribution
family is fixed for each task; it is not a hypothesis-search dimension.
The task interface supplies this likelihood and the link or forward model. Prediction uses the response-space conditional mean,
not the raw expression when the link is nonlinear.
ChemBench, BoxingGym, and NewtonBench allow
+,−,×,/,∗∗+,-,\times,/,** and exp, log, sqrt,
tanh, square, and power, with at most six
expression coefficients and fifteen operators. ForceBench additionally
allows cos and sin, but its engine enforces three
free constants and ten operators. Noise parameters are not counted as
expression coefficients.

Validation checks syntax, coefficient roles, consistency with the fixed
task likelihood, and finite predictions under 64 prior draws (on BoxingGym, at 16 probe
inputs). Canonicalization normalizes expression syntax, roles, and
family, including equivalent power notations. ForceBench also checks
numerical duplicates on a probe grid. These online duplicate tests are
not the full structural judge of Appendix C.
Rejected proposals and failed fits retain their failure reasons rather
than receiving an artificial evidence value.

Proposal and pool settings.

The proposer uses Claude Opus 5.5 with medium reasoning effort,
16,000 output tokens, and a 120-second timeout. SDK retries are disabled;
a validation failure permits one repair turn. Initialization requests
12 candidates and permits two refill waves. Later rounds request three
candidates without refill. The pool retains up to 12 valid fitted model
families by evidence, with equal family-level prior weights. Every fifth round, the proposer may nominate up to two
retired candidates; each is refitted from its original prior on all
current data before competing for retention. All failed proposals and
retirements remain in the archive.

Point predictions average conditional response means over these

Joint-EIG estimation and search.

The estimator samples a family, posterior parameters, and a hypothetical
outcome, then compares its conditional likelihood with the weighted
predictive mixture. Density calculations use log-sum-exp arithmetic.
Invalid components are tracked; an all-invalid query is a failure, not
zero information. Negative finite-sample estimates are not clipped.
The component estimators appear in Appendix F.

ChemBench and NewtonBench start five searches uniformly in the scaled
unit box and select the best endpoint under the search draws. The
256-point pools declared for other comparison methods are not used by
these runs. ChemBench uses log coordinates for designated positive
inputs and affine coordinates otherwise. For iteration kk, its
random-sign finite-difference schedules are

ak=0.2(10+k)0.602,ck=0.05k0.101,a_{k}=\frac{0.2}{(10+k)^{0.602}},\qquad c_{k}=\frac{0.05}{k^{0.101}},

and starts compass search from the best five points; it re-evaluates
the pool maximum and search endpoints together. Survival instead scores
every unobserved patient.

ForceBench uses five uniform starts and Adam ascent. One set of random draws is shared across
all starts and steps within a round; endpoints are re-estimated with
independent draws before selection. Every search respects the design
constraints and keeps the fitted model pool fixed.

String identity is unnecessary. For positive domains,
a​x/(b+x)=x/(θ1+θ2​x)ax/(b+x)=x/(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}x) with

Equivalence and containment checks.

A deterministic judge (version 1.3) first applies the task’s link, input
transformations, and target conventions. It uses neither an LLM nor the
agents’ held-out prediction scores.
Strict equivalence requires matching canonical expression trees,
a coefficient mapping solved in both directions, a 30-digit numerical
spot check, and admissible coefficients reproducing the true and
diagnostic parameter settings. Canonicalization splits exponentials of
sums and logarithms of products, merges powers, normalizes fractions,
extracts leading coefficients, and applies trigonometric addition
formulas. Diagnostic settings comprise the true setting plus two
additional settings on ChemBench, NewtonBench, and ForceBench, or three
on BoxingGym.

The judge marks non-equivalence for incompatible input dependence,
coefficient-Jacobian rank, or coefficient domains, or for failure to
reproduce a target member within 10−310^{-3} under an eight-start refit.
Containment is recorded separately. Its checks include up to
60 simplifying substitutions (coefficients set to 00, 11, or a
limiting ∞\infty, or tied together), a solved mapping into a
higher-dimensional family, and wider coefficient domains. When no
symbolic witness is found, a numerical check fits diagnostic members
and requires error within 10−610^{-6} on separate check inputs.
This last check is evidence of numerical representability on those
inputs, not a symbolic proof of inclusion everywhere.

Symbolically unproved, numerically ambiguous, timed-out, or
non-identifiable cases remain unresolved. They fail strict
recovery but can pass the separate containment check. Thus counting
strict certificates is conservative; failure to obtain a certificate does not itself
prove non-equivalence. These truth-assisted checks are evaluation-only and
never alter the discovered candidates or their fitted predictions.

Appendix D Numerical Evaluation on Shared Candidates

Purpose and data.

Section 4.3 separates explicit prediction, parameter inference,
and model selection from differences in hypothesis generation and
experimental design. For each world–seed pair, we collect 20
random-design observations, denoted by 𝒟\mathcal{D}. This budget applies

separate from the sequential budgets in Table 3.
The reported cohort contains 90 ChemBench, 27 BoxingGym, 30 ForceBench,

and evaluation inputs within each run. No additional observations or

Candidate construction.

selection; it does not select that family on behalf of any method.
All methods then receive the same list and use all 20 observations.
The pool can differ between world–seed pairs but is fixed across A–D
within a pair. Likelihood families are fixed per task, as in
Section 3.1.

whereas B produces an executable model. Thus A–B compares direct
numerical prediction with prediction through an explicit model, not
an isolated change to the LLM’s internal representation.

the corresponding response is observed. Residuals use each model’s
predictive scale. Even with explicit belief fields removed, residuals
can reveal relative fit quality, and pool membership reflects numerical
selection. The ablation therefore tests these feedback bundles, not the
elimination of all indirect evidence about candidate quality.

Initialization and execution.

Figure 6 compares nine ChemBench tasks and five
ForceBench worlds, each with three seeds (27 and 15 runs per variant).
Within each task–seed group, all four variants start from the same
initial observations 𝒟0\mathcal{D}_{0}, initial candidate pool, and fitted
state, including parameter posteriors and evidence estimates. A saved
round-0 state is replayed to keep this initialization identical.
From the first feedback-guided proposal onward, each variant follows
its own trajectory: it proposes families, chooses experiments, obtains
observations, and updates its fitted pool. Likelihoods, priors, grammar,
pool capacity, proposal counts, and archive-review eligibility are
otherwise kept fixed.

This is a sequential feedback ablation, not the fixed-data comparison
of Section 4.3. Subsequent datasets and model pools need not
match, even though initialization and the numerical inference and
design procedures are shared.

Evaluation and interpretation.

The horizontal axis is the total number of observations. ChemBench
uses observation-mean nMSLE, and ForceBench uses trajectory nMSE on
the shared evaluation designs. The target definitions and geometric
aggregation follow Appendix A. The ChemBench
panel displays observations 13–33; ForceBench displays 2–18.

In Figure 6, Full has the lowest final error on
both benchmarks, while No feedback has the highest. Either feedback
bundle generally improves prediction over No feedback. These results
support returning numerical beliefs and residual diagnostics to the
LLM, rather than using numerical inference only to score its existing
hypotheses. Because proposals and observations subsequently evolve,
the measured effect is on downstream prediction in the discovery loop,
not an isolated score of individual proposal quality. The plotted bands
summarize seed dispersion, not confidence intervals for paired gains.

Appendix G Proposer Prompt Components

The proposer combines the supplied full-feedback base instruction
(FA–C-R) with the three-candidate and archive-review extensions below.
Each request also supplies the task interface, observations, feedback,
and task-specific constraints. The base asks for one candidate; the
composition extension overrides this output format with
{limit} set to three. FA–C-R provides residuals for all
active models. The auxiliary comparison in
Appendix F uses the older FA–C base and earlier
extensions, which differ from the current full-feedback configuration.

Task constraints and terminology.

In the supplied prompt schema, the field named family denotes
the observation-distribution family, whereas “model family” in the
paper denotes the functional form. The experiments described in
Section 3.1 hold the observation-distribution family
fixed per task. Accordingly, the schema’s conditional support for
family_expansion does not make likelihood-family search part
of the reported method: this flag is disabled, and family
must match the task’s fixed distribution.

Task constraints also specify coefficient roles, expression operators,
and the link or forward model. ForceBench includes cos and
sin; the engine applies its task-specific limits of three
free constants and ten operators. The generic base text below includes
the six-coefficient/fifteen-operator limits used for the other interfaces.
The task-specific constraint block and validation enforce the applicable
limits. Parameter priors are assigned by coefficient role rather than
written numerically by the LLM.

Initialization and feedback variants.

Discovery initialization requests candidate forms from the initial
observations, with the candidate/refill settings in
Appendix B. The GT-containing pool of
Section 4.3 is a separate controlled construction, not a prompt
used to initialize the end-to-end discovery runs. In the feedback
ablation, all four variants replay the same initial fitted state before
receiving their respective feedback bundles.
Belief-only removes model-wise residuals; Diagnostics-only removes
belief fields and their ranking cues; No feedback removes both bundles.
The masking covers structured fields, accompanying prose, and history
as specified in Appendix E. The observed data,
candidate identities, and modeling interface remain available.

Full-feedback base prompt.

⬇

# prompt_version: v2.9.FA-C-R

Propose one new candidate model for the process described only by the data and the structured fields in the user message. A Bayesian engine fits and scores every candidate.

The user message is a JSON belief packet: interface (outcome type and support, input names and ranges, the fixed link), observations (all public data so far), pool (each active model with expression, family, evidence weight, log evidence, one-step predictive log-score history, and posterior mean and sd of each coefficient), residuals_by_model (for every active model, the standardized residual of each public observation under that model’s posterior predictive, keyed by model id and sorted by input), archive (every earlier proposal in this run with its outcome), and constraints. Nothing in the packet comes from held-out data. A large fitted dispersion makes standardized residuals look small even when the mean form is wrong.

Propose the one (expression, family) pair that you judge most likely to have generated all of the observations and that is not in the pool or the archive; if your first choice is already there, propose your next. Judge a form by how much of the data it would leave unexplained, not by how few edits separate it from the current best model. Inputs may combine through products, ratios or nested functions as well as through sums of separate terms.

Link

- Under a log link, expression is log(mean / response_unit): a factor f of the mean enters expression as + log(f) and a divisor g as - log(g). Inside log a raw input with a wide positive range is safe; outside log, such an input times a coefficient, or raised to a coefficient power, can overflow exp and is rejected.

Checks

- predicted_diagnostic: where your model should fit the observations better than the current best model, as {"input": <one input name>, "low": <number>, "high": <number>}. The two fitted models are compared on the observations whose value of that input lies between low and high.

- closest_archived_model: the expression in the pool or the archive that is most similar to yours, copied exactly.

Rules

- expression is the argument of the fixed link. Use only coefficient names declared in parameters, the input names given in interface, real literals, the operators + - * / **, and exp, log, sqrt, tanh, square, power(x, k). At most 6 coefficients and 15 operators.

- Count operators as the engine does: each +, -, *, /, ** and each unary minus counts 1, and each call of exp, log, sqrt, tanh, square or power counts 1; names and numbers count 0. For example c*tanh(w) counts 2, and each further term c*f(x + k) added to a sum costs 4. If your count is above 15, simplify the expression before replying.

- parameters maps every coefficient used in expression to exactly one of: intercept, signed, positive, shape (at most one intercept). Priors are fixed by these labels; never write numeric priors. Family dispersion parameters belong to the family; never list them.

- family must be one of constraints.allowed_families. If constraints.structure_expansion is false, expression must be verbatim one of constraints.initial_structures and only family may change. If constraints.family_expansion is false, family must equal constraints.initial_family.

- Your whole reply is one raw JSON object - its first character is { and its last is } - with the keys {"expression": ..., "family": ..., "parameters": {...}, "predicted_diagnostic": {...}, "closest_archived_model": ...}: no code fence and no text before or after it. If it fails validation you receive the error once and may reply once more.

Three-candidate extension.

⬇

Return a JSON array of exactly {limit} candidate objects, each with exactly the keys specified above and nothing else. Propose three distinct, non-equivalent hypotheses. At least two of the three candidates should differ meaningfully in their primary functional composition, rather than merely in parameterization or by adding or removing a correction term. At most one candidate may be a nested or incremental refinement of a model already in the pool, and only if the public observations specifically call for that refinement.

Archive-review extension.

Every fifth round, the extension below also allows up to two retired
model IDs. Each nominated model is refitted from its prior on current
data, rather than restored with an old evidence value.

⬇

This is an archive review round. Besides your {limit} new candidates, you may ask for up to two archived models to be re-fit: choose only among archive entries whose outcome is retired and whose form you now judge likely given all current public observations. Each model you name is re-fit from its prior on all current public data and takes part in the pool like any other model. Reply with one raw JSON object {"candidates": [...], "revive": [...]}: candidates is the array described above, and revive lists the chosen archive model_id strings exactly as given, or is empty when nothing in the archive deserves another fit.
