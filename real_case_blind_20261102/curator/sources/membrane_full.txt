1.IntroductionSoft machines [1, 2] hold great promise for autonomous operation in real‐world scenarios [3]. The intrinsic compliance of their bodies leads to features that distinguish them from rigid machines, such as passive adaptability [4, 5, 6], exhibition of complex output deformations from simple inputs [7], and resilience to damage [8, 9].Relatively recently, fluidic circuits [10] have been identified as a promising platform to increasingly move autonomy capabilities from a central electronic processor to the machine's soft body itself, a practice denoted as physical control [11]. Fluidic circuits, consisting of networks of centimeter‐scale tubings [12, 13, 14] and nonlinear inflatable elements [15, 16], allow for internal sequencing of complex motions given simple pneumatic inputs. For example, given a cyclic pressure drive, completely fluidic robots sequence leg movements to locomote [17], and even play Beethoven's Ninth Symphony [18].To achieve such functionalities, these systems [17, 18] require an externally timed pressure signal. To increase the independence from external computers, fluidic circuits can generate their own timing signals internally, harnessing self‐oscillation [19]. Given a constant input, self‐oscillating fluidic circuits [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27] embedded in the machines' bodies generate oscillatory pressure signals, sequencing the actions of the machines. For example, fluidic relaxation oscillators obtain cyclic activation given a constant inflow of air through a single hysteretic valve [23, 26]; the valve alternates between a closed and an open state, snapping between each state due to its nonlinear characteristic curve. Fluidic ring oscillators [28], given constant input pressure, display a pressure wave that travels along a ring of three interconnected valves, sequentially inflating limbs of a locomoting robot [29]. In our previous work, self‐oscillating limbs made of kinked elastic tubes display cyclic, asymmetric motions when connected to an external mini air pump, resulting in responsive, synchronized gaits across various environments [20].In general, fluidic circuits rely on mechanical nonlinearities to self‐oscillate, where hysteretic behavior arises from microfluidic networks [30, 31] or macroscopic elements with specific geometric designs. For instance, the relaxation oscillator [23, 26] relies on a shell with a non‐monotonic pressure‐volume curve and with a cut at its pole [32]; the ring oscillator [28] requires multiple bistable valves, each assembled from a bistable shell, an external soft capsule, and two kinking tubes [21]; the self‐oscillating limb leverages a thin elastic tube along which kink instabilities spontaneously form, travel, and disappear [20].These self‐oscillations are passive, as they rely on an external pneumatic power supply, often in the form of a compressor. Even when untethered, the power supply is a separate component placed onboard as relatively bulky pumps and batteries [20, 22] or pressure canisters [25, 29]. To remove rigid parts associated with the power supply, researchers have harnessed liquid fuels that rapidly decompose into gases when in contact with a catalyst [33, 34], directly inflating soft limbs [30]. However, even in this scenario, the energy system is external to the passive self‐oscillating system, which still requires a relatively complex network of gates and microfluidic components [31] to generate cyclic activation.Outside the realm of fluidics, self‐shadowing effects are commonly used to obtain oscillations in carefully designed materials. For instance, strips of photoactive polymers undergo self‐oscillations when illuminated with constant light [35, 36, 37, 38]. In chemo‐mechano‐chemical homeostatic materials, catalytic microstructures reversibly move into and out of a reactant layer when their supporting hydrogel swells and deswells, thereby regulating chemical reactions that, in turn, feed back on the hydrogel swelling state, closing a self‐regulating loop [39]. Self‐oscillating polymer brushes as artificial cilia use Belousov–Zhabotinsky redox waves to drive autonomous nanoscale swelling‐deswelling cycles with controlled unidirectional propagation [40]. In general, in these systems, the stimulus‐induced activation leads to shape changes that cause subsequent suppression of the stimulus, effectively resetting the system for the next cycle. Crucially, in these systems, the access to the energy source is directly dependent on the internal states of the oscillating dynamics. In other words, the energy‐consuming feature typical of active matter [41, 42, 43, 44]—for which energy gets dissipated internally—enters directly as a state variable of the oscillators. Therefore, here we refer to such systems as active oscillators.Such coupling between energy access and internal states allows mechanisms with relatively simple responses (for instance monotonically bending beams [45, 46]) to undergo out‐of‐equilibrium oscillations. This active oscillation architecture contrasts with passive fluidic self‐oscillators, which instead require elements with highly nonlinear mechanical responses, such as mechanical instabilities [20, 22, 23, 25, 29, 30]. We hypothesize that this reliance on nonlinear mechanical elements arises because the passive oscillating subsystem is decoupled from the subsystem providing the energy input. On the other hand, compared with active self‐oscillating materials, passive self‐oscillating fluidic circuits have a distinct feature: their output is an oscillatory pressure or flow signal that can directly drive the actuation and sequencing of soft devices to perform specific functions [11], such as locomotion [20], manipulation [47], and self‐sensing [48].Here, we aim to bridge these two approaches and combine the advantages from both. We present a soft system to study active oscillations in the context of soft fluidic machines. We couple an elastic membrane with a catalytic chemical reaction of liquid hydrogen peroxide that decomposes into gases. We observe that this coupling alone leads to oscillations of the reaction, and thereby of the membrane, that self‐sustain for multiple consecutive hours. By leveraging nonlinearities in the interaction between the liquid and the catalyst, the pressure oscillation does not require membranes with complex, nonlinear responses, intricate fluidic circuits, or external power sources. We show with a lumped‐parameter model that the dynamic inflation‐deflation cycle arises from an interplay between the membrane stiffness, venting resistance, and capillarity of the catalyst. Importantly, in this system, the release of energy is directly coupled to the oscillator itself. Hence, we refer to this model system as an active fluidic oscillator that embodies energy [49]. Note that we use the term embodied energy in the soft‐robotics sense, referring to energy that is physically stored within the body of the machine and directly coupled to its dynamics [49], rather than to the lifecycle‐assessment meaning of the energy required to produce a good or service.

At atmospheric pressure, a small volume of liquid fuel decomposes into a large volume of gas, with a volume increase of ∼250 times at 50% concentration by weight in water [51]. 3.The Active Oscillations Self‐Sustain for HoursTo systematically study the active oscillation, we prepare the experiment by inflating the membrane so that the embedded catalyst is displaced away from the pool, as shown in Figure S4. This allows us to fill the pool with 6mL of fuel without triggering the reaction. After this setup procedure, we start the experiment by venting the chamber to the atmosphere through the on/off valve (Figure S4). We define the start of the experiment (t=0s) when the chamber reaches atmospheric pressure. After this initial manual interaction, we let the system evolve over time, without further external interactions.In the first minutes of the experiment, we observe the start of an active oscillation of the membrane, which alternatively inflates and deflates between 10 and 20kPa at a frequency of ∼0.06Hz (Figure 2a and Movie S1). 4.A Lumped‐Parameter Model of the Active OscillationMotivated by the observation that oscillations last for hours consecutively, we further investigate the key mechanism behind the active oscillation. In particular, we know that for an active oscillation to self‐sustain, a form of positive or negative feedback [52], switching nonlinearity, or delay within the internal states of the system is required [19]. We sought to elucidate the source of the active oscillation in our specific system. We hypothesize that the active oscillation is sustained by a combination of effects. (1) The catalyst holds fuel via capillary forces even when it has emerged from the pool, implying that the reaction can continue even when the catalyst is not in contact with the pool surface. (2) The reaction occurs more slowly than the fuel wicks into the catalyst, and venting of gases to the atmosphere is slower than the rate of generation of gases, inducing an effective sequence of events with wicking first, then reaction, then venting. (3) The generation of reacted gases inflates the membrane, which consequently displaces the catalyst away from the fuel pool, hence acting as a negative feedback effect.To understand the mechanism behind the active oscillation, and in particular to uncover the effects of various physical parameters on the outcome behavior, we develop a model that captures the physical contributions to the system (Figure 4 and Supplementary Materials). With the model, we aim for a qualitative, yet informative, description of the overall system. Hence, the model couples the influence of the reaction kinetics, the effects of the catalyst temperature, the capillarity of the catalyst, the mechanics of deformation of the membrane, and the leakage through the venting resistor. The model, which lumps complex physical phenomena into relatively simple relationships, qualitatively captures the experimental results and offers insights into the key phenomena that enable the active oscillation to occur. Specifically, the model teaches us that the active oscillation arises from a time offset induced by the catalyst capillarity and negative feedback resulting from the catalyst being physically attached to the inflating membrane.FIGURE 4Schematic of the model of the active oscillator. Importantly, this is implicitly describing a hysteretic (hence nonlinear) interaction between fuel and membrane, for which the mass of fuel in the catalyst is not uniquely defined over the catalyst displacement. For instance, while the catalyst holds fuel when lifted and moving away from the pool, it may be empty on its way back toward the pool. We assume that the mass of hydrogen peroxide attached to the silver catalyst mf,cR decomposes into only gas mass mggen (water vapor and oxygen gas) without any residual liquid water. In reality, only some of the generated water evaporates due to the high temperatures around  and some stays in liquid form; however, it is not obvious to quantify the amount of residual liquid water, so we neglect this contribution and we assume all water evaporates for simplicity. The hydrogen peroxide decomposition has been described as a first‐order catalytic reaction [53]:
m˙ggen(t)=kr(Tc(t))·mf,cR(t).This reaction is known to be temperature‐dependent, where the rate kr at which the reaction occurs is exponentially increasing for higher temperatures Tc following the Arrhenius equation [54]. Since we are aiming for a qualitative (yet informative) description of the overall coupled system, for simplicity, we assume the reaction rate to be linearly increasing with the catalyst temperature Tc:
(2)kr(Tc(t))=kr0+Cr·(Tc(t)−T0),with T0 the ambient temperature, kr0 the baseline reaction rate at ambient temperature, and Cr the reaction constant, that is the slope of the reaction rate function of catalyst temperature. In real catalytic hydrogen peroxide decomposition, the self‐accelerating Arrhenius relationship is bounded by more complex physical phenomena such as evaporative cooling [55, 56], or heat and mass transfer between the liquid and gas phases [55]. Without these additional mechanisms modeled, an Arrhenius term for reaction rate is known to risk a runaway reaction in modeling, creating a non‐physically accurate ‘explosion’ in system temperature [57]. Thus, the choice of a linear reaction rate relationship with temperature captures the necessary temperature dependence without the runaway risk, making it the more physically appropriate choice within the scope of our oscillator model.The inflating membrane does not operate in the snap‐through [15] regime, that is, the pressure‐displacement curve is fairly linear and does not present negative stiffness regions [58, 59] in the observed deformation range. The spring compresses vertically by a displacement u (which we refer to as the catalyst displacement) under the force Fp that originates from the pressurized gas inside the chamber. We assign physically plausible values to the parameters as summarized in Table S1, where we indicate for each parameter if it is either scientifically well established, experimentally measured by us, or resulting from a qualitative fit so that the simulations qualitatively match the experiments in Figure . We integrate forward the set of ODEs in Equation S25 (initialized as in Equation S30) with the MATLAB ode23s solver, which is effective in solving stiff ODEs through a modified Rosenbrock method [61, 62]. The parameters used in these simulations are listed in Table S1.Importantly, we find that a key phenomenon enabling the active oscillation is this time offset between the catalyst gaining reactable fuel and the effective inflation of the membrane. This time offset can be better understood by looking closely at the third inflation cycle (Figure 5c,d) (avoiding the transient behavior of the first cycles). When the catalyst dips into the fuel pool (green), it gains reactable fuel via effective fuel uptake (Figure 5d, blue arrow). Pressure marginally rises because some of the gained fuel in the catalyst reacts, and importantly, it keeps rising (Figure 5c, red arrow) even after the catalyst exits the pool (purple), as a consequence of the fuel present in the catalyst reacting and turning into gases (Figure 5d, red arrow), even when the catalyst is no longer dipped in the pool.To better understand one complete cycle, we plot pressure against reactable fuel in the catalyst mf,cR (Figure 5e), noticing a system‐level hysteretic behavior. When dipped, mf,cR increases while pressure drops initially (blue arrow). When the reaction picks up in the catalyst, causing pressure to increase (small red arrow), the catalyst has already gained mf,cR≈12mg. After the catalyst exits the pool, this fuel held by capillary forces continues to react, hence pressure increases while mf,cR decreases (red arrow), until mf,cR=0. With all fuel reacted, the gas generated vents to atmosphere (grey arrow) and the catalyst cools back down (Figure 5c), bringing the system back to its initial submerged state to begin another cycle.Hence, this model teaches us that the active oscillation arises from two sources: (1) a time offset between fuel entering the catalyst in liquid form and leaving the catalyst in the form of gases, and (2) negative feedback between pressurization and reaction. The first contribution, the time offset, is a consequence of (1) the catalyst being able to hold liquid on its surface through capillary forces, even when not dipped in the pool, and (2) the faster timescale of fuel uptake into the catalyst with respect to the timescale of the reaction, and the faster reaction than venting. Together, these effects result in a causal sequence of events, where first the fuel uptake occurs, then the reaction happens, then venting takes over. The second contribution, negative feedback, results from the catalyst being physically attached to the membrane, which displaces upward when pressurized, hence distancing the catalyst from the pool of fuel.Notably, the slower timescale of the catalytic reaction relative to fuel delivery is also supported by our previous independent experiments with the same silver‐wire catalyst and fuel [34]. There, pressurization of a cold reactor exhibits a warm‐up delay consistent with the temperature‐dependent decomposition rate [34]. In addition, a faster fuel injection yields a less complete reaction due to reduced residence time [34]. Both these observations indicate that the fuel reaches the catalyst faster than it decomposes.The time offset arising from the causally sequenced fuel uptake, reaction, and venting, and the negative feedback are apparent when we represent the model in a block diagram (Figure 5f) where the key variables either inhibit or promote each other, inspired by the representations often used in molecular biology [63, 64]. We notice how fuel uptake promotes the mass of fuel in the catalyst (Figure 5f, blue), which in turn promotes the generation of gases through the reaction (Figure 5f, red), which causes pressurization and promotes the catalyst displacement u to increase. In turn, the catalyst displacing upwards causes a decrease in the surface area of contact As of the catalyst with the fuel in the pool, inhibiting fuel uptake to the catalyst, thereby acting as a negative feedback mechanism (Figure 5f, green). Looking at the phase diagram in Figure S7, where we analyze the system's response to positive and negative feedback strengths, it is evident that the active oscillation is sustained when the negative feedback effects balance the positive feedback effects.In addition to explaining the active oscillation behavior, the model also captures the long‐term evolution of the oscillation in Figure 3. In particular, the model captures the decrease of the maximum and minimum oscillation pressures in time (Figure S7a, blue). This is a consequence of the available reactable fuel in the pool being depleted, as indicated by the concentration of fuel in the pool dropping from αp=0.42 to αp=0.07 over the course of 8 simulated hours (Figure S8a7a, dark red). The reduced amount of available reactable fuel effectively results in the system reacting less and less, as indicated by the maximum catalyst temperature dropping to ambient temperature (Figure S8b). When this condition is reached after approximately 8 hours, the time scale of gas generation is no longer shorter than that of gas venting, and the system stops oscillating, as also observed in experiments (Figure 3).
6.Influence of Parameters on the Active OscillationHaving learned that the separation of timescales and the negative feedback intrinsic to our system are the sources of the active oscillation, we wonder how the system responds to variations in key physical parameters. In particular, we vary parameters that can be tuned by design, such as membrane stiffness and venting resistance, to confirm that the model captures a change of behavior that can be observed experimentally. Then, we investigate how the behavior of the modeled system changes when we vary the parameters intrinsic to the reaction, such as catalyst capillary capacity and reaction constant. 7.DiscussionWe introduced a system that undergoes active oscillations by harnessing the coupling between the deformation of an elastic membrane and a catalytic reaction. We observe oscillations in which the access to and release of energy are directly coupled to internal states of the system, effectively embodying the energy source within the oscillator itself. Through a lumped‐parameter model, we learned that the key phenomena underlying the oscillation are the negative feedback resulting from the catalyst being attached to the deforming membrane and the internal time offsets between fuel uptake, reaction, and venting, which arise from the three different timescales.The model qualitatively captures the oscillation dynamics, and it reproduces the experimental results when varying parameters. Hence, the model both enables the understanding of the phenomena at play and can prove useful to provide design guidelines for oscillating soft robots. However, the current model follows a number of simplifications, which in future work can be extended to capture more details of the experiments. For instance, we model the reaction rate as linearly dependent on the catalyst temperature, while in reality, an exponential trend is expected [54]. Moreover, we assume that the chamber stays at ambient temperature, while experiments show that it can rise (Figure 3d). This assumption is the likely cause of the increase in frequency at the beginning of the experiment (Figure 3a), which our model does not capture: the model only describes the quasi‐steady‐state of the system, failing at capturing the initial transient. Our simplifications also account for the main quantitative discrepancies between model and experiment. The model overestimates pressure (Figure S9) mainly because we assume that all reaction water evaporates, so the generated gas comprises both oxygen and water vapor (up to three moles of gas per two moles of hydrogen peroxide); experimentally, a fraction of the water stays liquid, lowering the gas mass and the pressure. The constant‐chamber‐volume assumption, which neglects the pressure relief from membrane inflation, also contributes to overestimating pressure. Conversely, the model underestimates the minimum catalyst temperature (Figure S8b), because we fix the heat sink at ambient temperature in the heat‐loss term (Equation S18), whereas in reality the chamber heats up (Figure 3), thereby flooring the catalyst at a higher minimum. Because the model parameters were fitted to qualitatively reproduce the early‐time oscillation of Figure 2 rather than the magnitudes of Figure 3 (Table S1), it is best regarded as a qualitative tool to reveal underlying mechanisms. For quantitatively accurate, application‐specific design, the lumped description could be complemented by higher‐fidelity coupled thermo‐chemo‐mechanical numerical simulations that resolve membrane mechanics, Arrhenius kinetics, wicking, water phase change, and the spatial temperature field.Our model allowed for a prediction of the behavior of the oscillator when the catalyst capillarity and the reaction rate are varied (Figure 7). Although outside the scope of this work, future research could confirm these trends experimentally. In fact, the reaction constant could be varied experimentally by using fuel with a lower or higher concentration of hydrogen peroxide, or by adding sodium hydroxide to the 50% concentrated fuel, to change the pH and obtain a more reactive fuel. Moreover, custom manufacturing of the silver catalyst would allow fine‐tuning the amount of fuel that can be held through capillarity.The current design of the active oscillator serves the purpose of an experimental testbed that operates under controlled conditions, so that we can effectively isolate the various contributions and understand the phenomena at play. For functional operation in practical real‐world applications, we expect that the current prototype will require several changes. The device now relies on gravity to contain the fuel in the pool; other fuel storage solutions, such as soft porous materials like sponges [66, 67], would allow orientation‐independent operation. Moreover, the fabrication of the catalyst now follows a largely manual procedure (Supplementary Materials), hindering scalability and repeatability. Other manufacturing methods should be considered, such as casting or machining silver. By finely controlling the catalyst porosity at the fabrication stage, one could be able to derive a quantitative prediction of the detailed wicking dynamics [68] of the fuel within the porous catalyst, rather than resorting to an effective fuel‐uptake term as we did here. In addition, we observed relatively limited membrane displacement of ∼4mm upon oscillation (Figure 1g). Tuning design parameters (such as the membrane design) to obtain higher pressures (Figure 6) and, hence, higher displacements, will be relevant in future works aimed at soft robotic applications.For practical implementations, we note that the initial one‐hour transient (Figure 3) does not correspond to a non‐operational start‐up time, since the system is already actively oscillating during this period. Rather, the transient reflects the slow evolution of internal states such as chamber temperature. For applications requiring a prescribed frequency or amplitude from the start, future work could introduce additional physical feedback mechanisms to stabilize operation around a desired limit cycle.The relatively‐low‐frequency regime of our oscillator (0.008Hzto0.1Hz) suggests two routes toward soft robotic applications. Directly, it is suited to tasks that call for slow, sustained cyclic actuation without electronics, such as peristaltic and crawling locomotion, for which comparable electronics‐free systems operate at similar rates of a fraction of a hertz [29, 69]. Where faster actuation is instead required, the oscillator could act as a high‐level oscillatory power supply within a hierarchical architecture, in which the slow active oscillation feeds faster passive fluidic oscillators downstream, such as relaxation [23, 26], ring [28], or kinking [20] oscillators, which generate high‐frequency signals.Despite embodying energy, the proposed approach to achieve active oscillations in soft robotic systems consumes fuel in the process. Although we showed that 6mL of fuel is enough for the oscillations to sustain for more than 3 h consecutively, considerably longer than the minutes typical of pressurized canisters [29], our approach opens the question of refueling. Refueling would involve transportation of hydrogen peroxide in bulk from the manufacturing plant, where it is currently manufactured with energy‐intensive and centralized processes [70], to the site of operation of the robot. Besides being an impractical solution to the goal of energy‐autonomous robots, transportation, on‐site storage, and handling of hydrogen peroxide are hazardous, requiring the implementation of specialized storage and handling infrastructure in any facility wishing to use this technology [71, 72]. This would be a practical challenge to adoption, which may be softened through the future development of robotics‐friendly hydrogen peroxide devices and infrastructure, similar to the existing technology developed around other hazardous energy sources, such as lithium‐ion batteries or gasoline. In a real‐world scenario, where an untethered soft robot operates autonomously in the field, we envision a local approach to the synthesis of hydrogen peroxide directly from the water and air that are available in the surrounding environment. This approach has proven to be within reach, as researchers obtained concentrations of hydrogen peroxide up to 20% from water and oxygen through direct electrosynthesis [73]. However, considerable research effort is still required to obtain increased concentrations and to devise synthesis mechanisms that are embodied in the machine itself.With this preliminary and exploratory work, we show that relatively simple mechanical structures, such as flat elastic membranes, can undergo active oscillations when carefully coupled to other phenomena, such as capillary action and catalytic reactions. As such, we expand the library of phenomena that can be harnessed to achieve self‐oscillations in soft fluidic machines, providing additional routes to physically control soft robots while embodying energy.
